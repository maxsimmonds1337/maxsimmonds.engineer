# Hall Effect Thruster Market Analysis for VLEO/LEO Smallsat Startup

## Executive Summary

The 200–600 W power class targeting 50–250 kg satellites at 400–600 km LEO — with krypton or dual-propellant capability — is the most strategically defensible entry point for a new European HET startup. The gap is not in raw thrust class but in systems that combine: flight-qualified krypton operation, sub-600 W power class, fully integrated PPU+FMS turnkey delivery, and European supply chain independence from US ITAR restrictions.

## 2. What Power Classes Are Actually Needed? VLEO/LEO Drag Physics

### Atmospheric Drag at VLEO Altitudes

The drag force on a satellite is given by:

**F_drag = ½ × ρ × Cd × A × v²**

Where:
- ρ = atmospheric density (varies enormously with altitude and solar activity)
- Cd ≈ 2.2–3.7 in free molecular flow regime (VLEO)
- A = frontal cross-sectional area
- v ≈ 7.8 km/s (circular orbit velocity changes only ~2% between 200–400 km)

Key atmospheric densities (NRLMSIS 2.0 model, moderate solar activity):

| Altitude | Density (kg/m³) | Notes |
|----------|-----------------|-------|
| 200 km | ~3 × 10⁻¹⁰ | High solar activity can be 10× this |
| 250 km | ~6 × 10⁻¹¹ | |
| 300 km | ~2 × 10⁻¹¹ | Borderline survivable for sustained ops |
| 400 km | ~2 × 10⁻¹² | ISS altitude; manageable drag |
| 500 km | ~6 × 10⁻¹³ | LEO "standard" |
| 600 km | ~2 × 10⁻¹³ | Starlink/Kuiper altitude |

**Worked drag force examples** (Cd = 2.5, v = 7.8 km/s):

| Altitude | Satellite class | Mass (kg) | Frontal area (m²) | Drag force |
|----------|-----------------|-----------|-------------------|------------|
| 250 km | 6U CubeSat | 10 | 0.03 | ~0.5 mN |
| 250 km | 50 kg microsat | 50 | 0.15 | ~2.5 mN |
| 250 km | 150 kg smallsat | 150 | 0.4 | ~6.5 mN |
| 250 km | 300 kg smallsat | 300 | 0.7 | ~11 mN |
| 300 km | 150 kg smallsat | 150 | 0.4 | ~2.2 mN |
| 300 km | 300 kg smallsat | 300 | 0.7 | ~3.7 mN |
| 400 km | 150 kg smallsat | 150 | 0.4 | ~0.22 mN |

**Critical implication:** At 250 km, a 150 kg satellite needs ~6.5 mN of continuous thrust just for drag compensation. This drives propellant consumption significantly — at 1,400 s Isp, that's ~0.47 g/s of xenon equivalent for
continuous firing. Over a year of continuous operation that is ~15 kg propellant budget for drag alone.

The power required to deliver 6.5 mN at 1,400 s Isp with ~35% thruster efficiency works out to approximately **200–350 W** — well within the range of what a 150 kg smallsat can allocate. Typical power allocation for propulsion on
a 100–300 kg smallsat is **15–30% of total power budget**, which typically runs 300–800 W total for a modern LEO smallsat at this mass, giving **50–250 W available for propulsion** at standard LEO. At VLEO (250 km) the power
budget for drag compensation climbs to 200–400 W.

### Station-Keeping at Standard LEO (400–600 km)

At 500 km, drag on a 150 kg satellite is approximately 0.06–0.1 mN — negligible for continuous thrust. Here, the propulsion requirement is dominated by:
- **Orbit raising from injection altitude** (often 350–400 km) to operational altitude
- **Constellation slot maintenance** (±5–10 km precision)
- **Collision avoidance maneuvers** (multiple per year)
- **End-of-life de-orbit** (ESA/FCC requirements: <5 years to atmospheric reentry)

For a 150 kg satellite at 500 km needing 200 m/s total ΔV (orbit raising + lifetime station-keeping + deorbit), using the rocket equation at 1,400 s Isp: **propellant mass ≈ 22 kg** (~15% of satellite mass). This is achievable
with a 200–400 W thruster over a 1–3 year operational lifetime.

### What Power Class Do Operators Actually Want?

Based on the OneWeb/Busek case (BHT-350, 300 W nominal, 640 km orbit), the constellation standard for 150–200 kg satellites has converged on:

- **Power class: 200–600 W** (with 300–400 W as the sweet spot for 100–250 kg sats)
- **Thrust: 15–40 mN** (enabling orbit raising in weeks not months)
- **Isp: 1,200–1,600 s** (trade-off between thrust and efficiency; higher Isp preferred for long missions)
- **Total impulse: 100–500 kN·s** (for 3–7 year constellation lifetimes)
- **System mass: <5 kg** (thruster + PPU + propellant management)
- **Krypton compatibility: increasingly mandatory** for cost reasons

## 3. Market Gaps — Where Is the Unserved Niche?

### Gap 1: 300–600 W, Krypton-Native, Flight-Qualified, European

This is the primary gap. Analysis:

- **Busek BHT-350:** Xe/Kr capable, flight-proven, but **US product subject to ITAR**. European constellation operators (European Starlink alternatives, ESA-funded constellations, defence smallsats) have a strong policy preference
for non-ITAR supply chains.
- **Safran EPS-X00:** Kr-compatible, European, but **just entering market (first deliveries H1 2025)**. Still unproven at scale, targets >200 kg satellites, prices likely high (legacy GEO pricing culture), and Safran has no
manufacturing culture optimised for constellation volume pricing.
- **Exotrail Spaceware Mini:** The closest European competitor. 300–600 W, Xe+Kr, strong commercial traction. However: no public flight heritage for the Mini yet; company is primarily a "propulsion as a service" business model
(they operate thrusters remotely) which some customers don't want; Isp characterisation at <1,400 s is lower than Busek at same power.

**The gap:** A **300–500 W, krypton-primary Hall thruster system** with:
- Flight heritage (even one constellation contract)
- Isp >1,400 s on Kr (achievable — Kr gives ~150 s Isp bonus over Xe at equivalent conditions)
- System mass <4 kg (thruster + PPU)
- European-ITAR-free supply chain
- Priced for constellation economics (<€80k per system at volume)

No European supplier currently has a flight-qualified product in this class. Safran is 6–12 months from first deliveries, and Exotrail's Mini is on a similar timeline.

### Gap 2: 100–200 W True VLEO Drag Compensation (250–350 km)

The DARPA TALOS program and ESA AETHER project demonstrate institutional recognition that VLEO at 200–300 km demands a thruster optimised for continuous low-thrust drag compensation (not episodic orbit raising). Requirements:

- Continuous operation capability (high duty cycle, >5,000 hours)
- 2–15 mN continuous thrust
- High thrust-to-power ratio (more important than Isp for drag compensation)
- Air-breathing potential (longer term)

Current products with any VLEO heritage: Aliena MUSIC (10–100 W, 3.6 mN at 84 W, launched on ELITE 2025) — but Singapore-based. No European equivalent.

**The gap:** A VLEO-optimised HET (100–200 W, 5–15 mN, >1,200 s Isp, >5,000 hr lifetime) specifically designed for sustained drag compensation at 250–350 km. The magnetically shielded channel geometry (which reduces wall erosion
at high duty cycles) is essential here and only the higher-end products (Safran PPS series, Busek high-end) implement this. No sub-kW European HET product combines magnetic shielding with VLEO optimisation.

### Gap 3: Dual-Mode Propellant Flexibility (Krypton + Argon)

SpaceX proved argon is viable. Argon is ~100× cheaper than krypton and ~1,000× cheaper than xenon. The barrier is higher ionization potential (15.8 eV vs. 14.0 eV for Kr, 12.1 eV for Xe), which requires higher discharge voltage
and increases erosion. SpaceX's argon thruster achieves 2,500 s Isp at 4.2 kW with 2.1 kg mass — which is extraordinary and likely benefits from custom magnetics and channel geometry.

No commercial supplier currently offers a flight-qualified argon-capable HET for commercial sale. A product that runs on Kr as primary with tested Ar fallback capability would be differentiated.

### Gap 4: Integrated "Plug-and-Play" System at Constellation Economics

The PPU is often the cost and mass bottleneck. A typical sub-kW PPU for a Hall thruster:
- Mass: 2–5 kg
- Efficiency: 88–93%
- Input voltage: 22–34 V (28 V bus standard)
- Output: up to 400 V discharge

The challenge is that the PPU often costs as much as the thruster itself and is frequently sourced separately (Sitael sells thrusters; Busek sells thrusters; PPUs are separate procurement). Exotrail and ThrustMe provide fully
integrated systems (thruster + PPU + propellant management + software) — this is what the market wants. A new entrant offering a fully integrated system at <5 kg total mass with a constellation-compatible supply chain (50–100
units/year capacity) occupies a commercially distinct position.

## 4. Krypton vs. Xenon: The Detailed Cost Case

### Cost per kg (2024–2025)
- **Xenon:** $5,000–$12,000/kg (significant volatility; supply constrained by air separation plant capacity; ~0.09 ppm in atmosphere)
- **Krypton:** $2,100–$4,800/kg (more available; 1 ppm in atmosphere; byproduct of O₂/N₂ air separation)
- **Xe+Kr blend (byproduct of LOX production):** ~$300–$800/kg (15× cheaper than pure Xe; 2–3× cheaper than pure Kr)
- **Argon:** $7–$15/kg (essentially free from industrial air separation; 0.93% atmospheric concentration)

### Propellant Mass Required (300 kg satellite, 500 km LEO, 5-year mission)
- Orbit raising from 350 km + station-keeping + deorbit ≈ 250 m/s total ΔV
- At 1,400 s Isp: propellant mass ≈ ~54 kg (18% mass fraction)
- Xenon cost for 54 kg: **$270,000–$648,000**
- Krypton cost for 54 kg: **$113,000–$259,000**
- Blend cost: **$16,000–$43,000**
- Argon cost: **$378–$810** (essentially negligible)

For a 648-satellite constellation (OneWeb scale), krypton vs. xenon saves $100M–$400M in propellant cost over the constellation lifetime. This is why every major new player (Starlink, Kuiper, OneWeb via Busek) has moved or is
moving to Kr or cheaper alternatives.

### Performance Trade-offs on Kr vs. Xe (on same thruster)

Published data from BHT-1500 and SPT-100 testing:
- **Isp:** Kr gives ~100–190 s *higher* Isp than Xe at same power/voltage (lower atomic mass → higher exhaust velocity)
- **Thrust:** ~10–15% lower with Kr (lower atomic mass, lower propellant utilization efficiency)
- **Total efficiency:** 7–8% lower on Kr vs. Xe
- **Wall erosion:** 1.5–2× higher with Kr (higher ionization potential → more wall-grazing ions)
- **Net verdict for LEO constellation:** Kr is a clear win. The Isp bonus offsets the lower thrust for station-keeping. Erosion is manageable for 5,000–7,000 hr LEO lifetimes. Cost savings are massive.

## 5. Constellation Operator Requirements

### Starlink (SpaceX, ~6,000+ satellites operational, ~42,000 planned)
- **V1/V1.5:** ~550 km altitude; Kr Hall thrusters; ~60 mN, ~1,500 s Isp; power ~300–400 W estimated
- **V2 Mini:** ~550 km; Ar Hall thrusters; **170 mN, 2,500 s Isp, 4.2 kW, 2.1 kg**; massive improvement
- **Requirements derived:** >100 mN thrust for fast orbit raising (launch to operational in days not weeks); >2,000 s Isp preferred for fuel efficiency; <3 kg thruster mass at kW-class power; argon propellant mandatory for cost;
in-house manufacture only — not a market opportunity

### OneWeb / Eutelsat (648 satellites, 1,200 km altitude)
- Uses **Busek BHT-350** (300 W, 17 mN, 1,244 s Isp, Xe/Kr)
- Performance demonstrated: orbit raising from 450 km → 1,200 km, station-keeping, collision avoidance, deorbit preparation
- **Key procurement insight:** OneWeb switched from a European provider to Busek (US) due to delivery and production rate problems during rapid constellation build. This shows a willingness to go non-European when European
suppliers can't deliver at scale.
- **Repeat opportunity:** OneWeb is planning Gen 2 satellites (details TBD). Eutelsat's financial situation (significant debt) may pressure them toward lower-cost propulsion alternatives — a gap for a low-cost European provider.

### Amazon Kuiper (~3,236 satellites approved, 590–630 km altitude)
- Custom in-house krypton Hall thrusters
- Requirements: ±9 km altitude maintenance, orbit raising from injection, debris avoidance, end-of-life deorbit
- **Not a market opportunity** — in-house only

### Planet Labs (200+ Doves, 450–550 km altitude)
- Original Doves (3U/6U, ~5 kg): very limited propulsion (some had Enpulsion FEEP on technology demonstration units); many fly without propulsion
- **SuperDove:** Enhanced Earth observation smallsat, still mass-constrained (~6 kg), limited propulsion budget — mostly relying on natural orbit decay for deorbit compliance
- **Planet's propulsion gap:** They need drag compensation for long-term operations but their satellites are too small and power-constrained for conventional HETs. The 9 W propulsion budget cited in one study illustrates the
constraint.

### Telesat Lightspeed (~198 satellites, 1,000–1,325 km polar MEO/LEO)
- Uses Maxar-built satellites; propulsion details not publicly disclosed
- High-altitude LEO means drag is minimal; focus is on orbit raising and precision slot maintenance
- Estimated power class: 500–1,000 W

### New European Constellation Operators (Emerging Market)
- **SES O3b mPOWER:** 1,200 km MEO; Boeing-built; uses chemical + electric propulsion
- **Rivada Space Networks:** 600 km LEO; Airbus-built; planned 600 satellites
- **Kinéis (IoT constellation, France):** 25 satellites at 650 km; small propulsion; ThrustMe NPT30-I2 selected
- **Iridium Next:** Already complete; Aerojet chemical propulsion
- **Planned European sovereign constellations (IRIS², EU/ESA funded):** 290 satellites; procurement underway; European supply chain strongly preferred — **this is the primary opportunity for a European HET startup**

## 6. Strategic Recommendation for a European HET Startup

### Target Specification

Based on the gap analysis, the optimal product for a new European entrant is:

| Parameter | Target Specification | Rationale |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| Power class | 200–600 W (throttleable) | Covers 50–300 kg satellites at LEO; drag compensation at 250–400 km |
| Thrust | 10–35 mN | Station-keeping + orbit raising on same thruster |
| Isp (Xe) | 1,300–1,600 s | Competitive with BHT-350 and EPS-X00 |
| Isp (Kr) | 1,450–1,750 s | Kr bonus; should exceed Xe Isp |
| Propellants | Kr primary, Xe fallback | Kr is the constellation market standard |
| System mass | <4 kg (thruster + PPU + FMS) | Must beat Busek BHT-350 system mass |
| Lifespan | >7,000 hours | 5–7 year constellation lifetime |
| Total impulse | >300 kN·s | Competitive with Exotrail Mini |
| Form factor | Integrated PPU | "Plug and play" — what operators want |
| ITAR status | ITAR-free European | Mandatory for EU institutional customers |
| Target price | <€70k at volume (>20 units/year) | Below estimated Busek pricing |

### Differentiation Vectors (Ranked by Defensibility)

1. **European ITAR-free supply chain** — Institutional/government constellation operators (IRIS², EU defence) will require this. Safran has this but is expensive and slow.
2. **Krypton-primary design** (not krypton-tolerant) — Design the channel, magnetics, and cathode around Kr from the start; don't adapt a Xe thruster. This improves Kr efficiency by 3–5% over adapted designs.
3. **Magnetic shielding** — Essential for >5,000-hour Kr operation (higher erosion rate requires this). Adds design complexity but unlocks the 7-year lifespan market.
4. **Sub-4 kg fully integrated system** — The integration challenge is where European incumbents (Sitael, Safran) are weakest. Safran's EPS-X00 targets >200 kg satellites, implying a relatively heavy system.
5. **VLEO duty cycle certification** — The ELITE mission (Aliena, 2025) will produce the first public dataset on sustained VLEO HET operation. A European thruster that is tested and certified for sustained 250–350 km operation
(not just survival) would be 2–3 years ahead of the market.

### Power Class Decision: 300 W vs. 500 W vs. 1 kW

- **300 W:** Directly overlaps with BHT-350/Exotrail Mini. High competition, but proven demand. Best for near-term revenue.
- **500 W:** Slightly above the cluster. Covers a wider throttle range (200–600 W). Addresses Safran EPS-X00 market but with potentially better mass/cost. **Recommended primary target.**
- **1 kW:** Competes with Safran PPS-1350-E and Exotrail Mini at the top. Requires larger satellite bus power budgets (not all 100–200 kg sats can supply 1 kW to propulsion). Market is smaller but higher value per unit.

**Recommendation: Design a throttleable 200–600 W system with nominal operating point at 400–500 W.** This covers the full 50–300 kg smallsat market, allows VLEO drag compensation (lower power setting) and LEO orbit-raising
(higher power setting) on the same hardware. Position it as the krypton-native, magnetically-shielded, ITAR-free European answer to the BHT-350.

Where the market is going

The constellation race has already picked a winner power class: 300–600W. OneWeb uses the Busek BHT-350 (300W nominal), Starlink V2 is 4.2kW (but in-house), Kuiper is custom Kr. Every large LEO constellation at 500–1,200km has converged
on that 300–500W band for 100–300kg satellites.

But that market has Busek, Safran EPS-X00, and Exotrail Mini all arriving at the same time. It's getting crowded.

The underserved market is VLEO — specifically 200–350km — where nobody has a qualified European thruster designed for continuous drag compensation.

---
The 200km imaging constellation: power calculation

For a VLEO imaging satellite at 200km, using the drag equation:

F_drag = ½ × ρ × Cd × A × v²

At 200km (moderate solar activity):
 ρ ≈ 3×10⁻¹⁰ kg/m³
 Cd ≈ 2.5, v = 7,800 m/s

50kg sat, 0.10m² frontal area:  ~2.3 mN
100kg sat, 0.25m² frontal area: ~5.7 mN
200kg sat, 0.50m² frontal area: ~11.4 mN

At HIGH solar activity (density spikes 5–10×):
50kg:  11–23 mN
100kg: 28–57 mN   ← brutal
200kg: 57–114 mN  ← basically impossible to compensate

The implication: At 200km, only small satellites (≤50–80kg) are realistic for sustained operation with current thruster technology. This is actually why VLEO imaging constellations tend toward smaller, cheaper satellites (Aliena's ELITE
mission is a good example — small satellite, modest power budget).

Tiziano's 200W design — is it the right class?

For a 50–100kg satellite at 200–280km: yes, it fits.

A 200W thruster running on Krypton should produce roughly 10–15mN thrust at 1,300–1,500s Isp, which gives:
- Comfortable margin for a 50kg sat at 200km (2.3mN drag, 5–6× margin for solar activity spikes)
- Workable for a 100kg sat at 250km (2.2mN drag, 5–7× margin)
- Marginal for a 100kg sat at 200km in high solar activity (5.7–57mN drag — insufficient at high end)

The strategic sweet spot for Tiziano's 200W design:
- VLEO smallsat constellation (50–100kg, 220–300km altitude)
- Krypton-native from day one
- Magnetic shielding essential — Kr erosion at continuous duty cycle kills unshielded cathodes fast
- This is genuinely underserved in Europe — Aliena is the only current player and they're Singaporean
